Why this exists

Spanish-speaking patients are a large part of US healthcare, and their messages are often read through machine translation or by an English-first system. Some Spanish words look like English words but mean something else. In a widely cited case, intoxicado was read as "intoxicated" when the family meant the patient had been made sick by something he ate or took, and treatment was delayed. Translation tools still make that mistake.

What it does

  • Read Spanish: one model call reads the Spanish message and fills a fixed English intake form.
  • Translate first: the message is translated to English, then the form is filled from the translation. This is the usual "translate, then use the English system" design.
  • Pitfall guard: notes from medical-interpreting sources are added for known pitfall words. Two word families are kept out of the guard on purpose, so the benchmark shows whether it helps beyond the words it was given.
  • Every symptom, medication, allergy and condition must quote the patient's words. The page checks that each quote really appears in the message.

What it is not

  • Not a medical device and not for clinical use. The benchmark is synthetic and template-built, not real patient data.
  • The Spanish test messages were written with AI assistance and have not yet been reviewed by a native-speaking clinician or interpreter.
  • The public demo runs on a free-tier hosted model, so never type real patient information.
